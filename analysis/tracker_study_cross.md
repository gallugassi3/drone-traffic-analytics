## 2. Metric reconciliation: "16K+ ids" vs 1,818 unique ids (traffic2)

* `bytetrack_traffic2`: **1818 distinct output ids**, max id value **17309**.
* `bytetrack_traffic3`: **339 distinct output ids**, max id value **2619**.

The two numbers measure different things. In ultralytics ByteTrack (`trackers/byte_tracker.py`), `STrack.activate()` draws a fresh id from the global counter for **every new candidate track** (line 103), but sets `is_activated = True` only on frame 1 (lines 108-109); a candidate born later becomes visible in the output only after it is re-associated in a subsequent frame (`re_activate` / `update`, lines 120/151). Every detection that spawns a candidate and never matches again burns an id invisibly. On the zoom-out clip that is ~15.5K of the 17.3K allocated ids; the annotated video's on-screen labels show the counter's *height* (e.g. `#17245`), which is where the README's "16K+ ids in 23s" reading comes from - it is the max id value, not the number of tracks that ever appeared.

**Which to quote:** distinct output ids (1,818 in 23.5 s, median track 8 frames) is the honest churn metric - it counts tracks a viewer can actually see. The id-counter value (17.3K) additionally counts one-frame candidates and is implementation-specific (ByteTrack's eager id allocation); if cited at all it should be labelled "id allocations (including never-output candidates)". The README's phrasing "track identity churned (16K+ ids in 23s)" overstates output churn by ~10x and should be restated as ~1.8K visible tracks / 17K id allocations (bytetrack).

## 3. ReID fragmentation diagnosis (botsort_reid, static video)

| median box area bucket (px^2, GMC quartiles) | botsort_gmc tracks (median len) | botsort_reid tracks (median len) | extra reid tracks |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Q1 (smallest) | 80 (9) | 206 (2) | +126 |
| Q2 | 81 (5) | 142 (3) | +61 |
| Q3 | 81 (5) | 118 (5) | +37 |
| Q4 (largest) | 81 (15) | 88 (10) | +7 |

On the *static* clip, with_reid + model:auto grows the track count from 323 to 554 and drops the median track from 6 to 4 frames - and the table shows where: the excess concentrates in the smallest-box buckets. Mechanism (config + data, not embedding inspection): with `with_reid: True, model: auto`, BoT-SORT gates association on appearance similarity (`appearance_thresh: 0.8`) computed from detector features. The objects here are tiny, low-texture, top-down vehicles (cars ~35x17 px; per-track median box area ~874 px^2 with trucks included): embeddings from such crops are unstable frame to frame, so the appearance check keeps vetoing matches that IoU alone would accept, splitting continuous physical tracks into new ids. That is the opposite of what ReID is for, and it also costs a broken counted table (69 vs 70). ReID at this object scale needs a much lower appearance_thresh or a purpose-trained small-object embedder - or simply staying off.

## 4. Churn attribution on traffic2 (bytetrack)

Of 1818 bytetrack tracks, 331 have <3 observations (excluded), and **1402 tracks die before the clip ends**. Rough split by the box-area trajectory at death (thresholds stated in `scripts/tracker_cross_analysis.py`):

* **died at the size floor** (final area <= 200 px^2, ~14x14): 540 (39%)
* **shrank toward the floor** (final <= 50% of the track's own peak area and <= 400 px^2): 53 (4%)
* **mid-size losses** (died while comfortably sized - association/occlusion territory): 809 (58%)

Detector-driven deaths (first two classes): **593 of 1402 (42%)**; 51% of deaths fall in the second half of the clip (after the zoom-out has shrunk the scene).

Sensitivity note - this split is threshold-dependent and should be read as a rough attribution, not a precise one: the median box area *at death* is 240 px^2, versus 330 px^2 for all boxes in the clip - deaths skew toward small boxes, and many "mid-size" deaths sit just above the 200 px^2 floor cut. Widening the floor to 400 px^2 reclassifies 74% as detector-driven, but that bound is generous (400 px^2 exceeds the clip's median box, since the zoom-out leaves *everything* small). So the fair statement is: **roughly half of all track deaths (42% at a strict floor, more under looser cuts) happen at or near the detector's size floor**, which supports "the detector's small-object limit, not tracker association, is the first-order bottleneck on this clip" - but a substantial minority of comfortable-size losses remains, so the tracker is not blameless.
